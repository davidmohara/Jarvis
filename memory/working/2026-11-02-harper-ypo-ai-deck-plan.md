## 1. Narrative arc

**The one sentence:** You already use AI. The gap between you and the person getting ten times the value is not the model, it is three decisions: which work you point it at, how you brief it, and what you refuse to let it touch.

**Why this arc for this room.** A peer group that already uses AI does not need permission or explanation. It needs a diagnosis of why the returns feel thin, plus moves it can run on Monday. So the hour is built as three decisions, each with a framework behind it and a moment in front of it:

- **Selection.** Most of their AI use is pointed at demand limited work, where speed creates no new revenue. The task compression matrix fixes this in eight minutes.
- **Delegation.** They are typing at the model like an operator instead of briefing it like a new hire. Demo 1 makes the delta physical.
- **Boundaries.** They have no rule for what an agent may do alone, and no recovery protocol for when it goes wrong. This is where the money and the reputation leak.

The arc deliberately rejects the two obvious YPO talks: the "state of the AI market" update (they can read the Journal) and the "AI will not take your job" reassurance (they are not worried, they are busy). The promise is operational: by 17:00 you will have one task to hand off and one line you will not cross.

### Section notes

**1. The Gap Is Not the Tool (5 min).** No introduction of AI, no biography beyond one line. Open on the observation: everyone in this room uses it, and most of us use it like a faster typewriter. Put up the 95% stat and reframe it: MIT calls it a learning gap, which is a polite way of saying selection and integration, not model quality. Land the promise: three decisions, one hour, one thing to hand off by Friday. Title slide carries the hour's title only.

**2. Decision 1: Which Work (10 min).** Five minutes teaching the 2x2, four minutes of Prompt 1, one minute debrief. Teach it in their language, not a consultant's: compressible work crossed against whether the business actually wants more of the output. The two corners that matter for this room are the top right (compressible plus demand unlimited: RFP responses, proposals, diligence memos, pro formas) and the bottom right (non compressible plus demand unlimited: judgment, relationships, the client conversation). Name the bottom left explicitly as where budgets die: it is cool, it is negative ROI, and most AI programs spend their money there. Cite the MIT 95% as the consequence of scattering across all four corners.

**3. The Wall You Are About to Hit (6 min).** Speed run the eight stages, not as a maturity lecture but as a map with two walls: the tool wall at stages one and two (identity and permission, "I am a controller" versus "I do controlling") and the trust wall at stage four to five (verifiability, which is where agentic work stalls). **4. Decision 2: How You Brief It (10 min).** Demo 1, then the pattern behind it. The teaching point is one sentence: the highest value thing you can say to an agent is "what do you need to know?" ## 3. The moments

### Discussion prompts

**Prompt 1 (0:11, four minutes).** "Name the work in your business where more output actually means more revenue, and the work where it does not." Purpose: makes the matrix theirs. Pre-load one example per member so David can prompt if the room stalls: Lockton RFP and proposal responses, City Electric branch quotes and submittals, Cresta diligence memos, Beck development pro formas and investor updates, Westcott client reporting, Hazels new item and account setup. Watch for the moment someone names a task in the bottom left corner; that is the teaching beat.

**Prompt 2 (0:53, three minutes).** "What did you hand to AI this week that you used to do yourself, and what did you keep? Why?" Purpose: surfaces their actual permission line, and reveals whether it was chosen or accidental. Most will describe a line drawn by feel, not by rule. That is the setup for reversibility.

**Prompt 3 (0:58, two minutes, lightning round).** "One task you will hand over by Friday, and one thing you will never let an agent do." One sentence each, no discussion, David goes last so the room hears his answer before he sits down. Purpose: converts the hour into two commitments per person without a workshop.

### Demo 1: Brief It Like a New Hire (0:21, ten minutes)

**What David shows.** A fresh Claude conversation. First run: a realistic but thin ask, spoken aloud, something like "draft a one page update to my board on where we are with AI." Let it produce something competent and forgettable. Do not rescue it. Then open a new conversation and type the master prompt: "Before you write anything, ask me the questions you need answered to do this well. Ask up to seven, then stop and wait."

**Then answer three of them live, using answers the room gives.** Not seven. Three. This is the part that cannot be faked and cannot be pre-baked, because the answers come from the room. Then let it write.

**What it proves.** The delta between run one and run two is entirely the brief. Same model, same minute, same person. It also proves the counterintuitive move: the best prompt is often a question, and the fastest way to a good output is to let the machine interview you first.

**Why there is no prep risk.** The second run depends on room answers. There is nothing to break and nothing to rehearse. Fallback only if the network dies: a pre-run version in a second tab, clearly labeled as pre-run, which David can walk through as a screenshot.

**One trap.** Steve Idoux had this exact realization on his own last week and said so out loud in the Lockton meeting ("no one knew that"). If Steve jumps in, give him the floor for 30 seconds. It is stronger coming from him.

### Demo 2: Meeting to Memory (0:43, eight minutes)

**What David shows.** A local transcript file (recommended: the Oct 8 Becoming AI Native prep call, or the Oct 27 Lockton call with names and client specifics stripped) run through a standing brief that extracts decisions, owners, dates, and unresolved questions into one page. Ninety seconds of setup, two minutes of run, then read the output next to the messy source.

**What it proves.** The leverage is in the plumbing, not the prompt. Connectors, a standing brief, and a repeatable output format beat a clever one-off prompt every time. It also proves the ROI case for the busiest person in the room: the value is not in writing faster, it is in not losing the reasoning behind a decision.

**Why this demo and not the week brief.** A live calendar and email pull is the higher wow and the higher risk: network, MCP availability, and the chance of putting a peer's email on a shared screen. A local file is deterministic, fast, and shows the same mechanism. If David wants the week brief, run it as a pre-generated second tab and describe it rather than run it.

**Fallback.** If the laptop or projector misbehaves, the output page alone carries the point. Have it printed. A printed one-pager next to a printed transcript is a better prop than a live screen anyway.

## 5. Frameworks section

Four frameworks appear. The rule for all four: the framework is a lens on the screen for 90 seconds, and the room does the work. None of them gets a teaching section.

| Framework | What it is | One line pitch for the slide |
|---|---|---|
| **Task Compression x Demand Elasticity** | Two axes. Is the task compressible (can AI collapse the time)? Is demand for the output limited or unlimited? The four corners are highest ROI, role expansion or do not backfill, augmentation, and negative ROI. | "The only selection question that matters: if this got ten times cheaper, would the business want ten times more of it?" |
| **The 8 Stage Trust Ladder and the Two Walls** | Stages 1 through 8, from zero AI to orchestrating agent swarms. Two barriers: the tool wall at 1 to 2 (identity and permission) and the trust wall at 4 to 5 (verifiability of output). Adapted from Steve Yegge's developer agent evolution model. | "You are not behind or ahead. You are at a stage, and the wall in front of you is trust, not technology." |
| **Role Evolution: Doer, Reviewer, Director, Operator** | As trust rises, the human moves from doing the work, to reviewing drafts, to directing execution, to orchestrating a swarm. | "Your job already changed. Your org chart has not." |
| **The Wingman Rule** | The Boeing Loyal Wingman: fully autonomous, no cockpit, but the lead pilot is accountable for what it does. | "Accountability cannot be delegated to a machine. If 'the AI did it' becomes an acceptable sentence in your business, you just gave away all of it." |

**Framework that appears only as a line, not a slide:** the agentic landing zone, compressed to the puppy analogy (beer is free, a puppy is not; every agent is a puppy and someone has to feed it). It runs as one sentence inside the boundaries section, and it is the sentence this room will repeat.

**Framework deliberately excluded:** the AI economics material (seat pricing breaking, the rate case, the capital layer). It is David's strongest written work and it is the wrong altitude for a 60 minute operational talk to six owner operators. Save it for the blog or a keynote.

## 7. Risks and gaps

**Risks, ranked by damage:**

1. **The Steve overlap.** Highest social risk in the room and the easiest to convert into an asset. If it is not handled in the first ten minutes, the compression framework reads as recycled material to the one person who has already seen it. Handle it by naming him.
2. **Demo failure in front of six peers.** Not a technical embarrassment so much as a credibility tax: this audience will read a broken demo as evidence that the whole thing is fragile. Every demo needs a printed or pre-generated fallback, and David needs to say "I have this pre run, let me show you both" rather than debug on screen.
3. **Tone drift toward consultant.** The material is Improving's, and the temptation is to present it as a methodology. This room will punish polish. The mitigation is structural: three prompts, two demos, and speaker notes written in David's voice with the stories left in.
4. **Time.** 18:00 is a wall. The plan has no end buffer by design, so the demos are the flex: Demo 1 can lose four minutes, Demo 2 can lose three, and Prompt 1 can lose two.
5. **Client confidentiality.** The Lockton and Remington stories are the best material in the deck and both are identifiable to a Dallas peer group. Every client name, system name (Sage), and dollar figure gets stripped or genericized. "A Dallas insurance brokerage" is enough.
