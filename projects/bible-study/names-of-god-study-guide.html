A Biblically Grounded Study

The Names of God

יהוה · אֱלֹהִים · אֵל שַׁדַּי

Each name a window into the character of the one who spoke creation into being

"And those who know your name put their trust in you." — Psalm 9:10 (ESV)

Why the Names of God Matter

In ancient Hebrew culture, names were not labels — they were revelations

The Theology of Divine Naming

In the ancient Near East, to know a name was to know something essential about its bearer. A name was not a sound assigned for social convenience — it conveyed identity, character, and purpose. Nowhere is this more true than in the names God reveals about Himself throughout Scripture.

The biblical writers were careful and intentional in their use of divine names. Different names surface at different moments in the narrative — each one chosen to illuminate a specific facet of who God is in relation to His people at that point. When Hagar is abandoned in the wilderness and God speaks to her, she names him El Roi — "the God who sees me." This is not an abstract theological statement. It is a woman's cry of recognition: the invisible God is not blind to her suffering.

As the Blue Letter Bible notes: "In the Old Testament times, a name was not only identification, but an identity as well." And the BibleProject observes that El, Elohim, and similar terms are generic titles for deity that many cultures used — but the God of Israel claimed all of them while insisting He had a personal name: YHWH, revealed to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14).

This study is organized into three layers: the foundational names (El, Elohim, Yahweh, Adonai), the compound El-names (El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Roi, El Olam), the compound Yahweh-names (Jehovah Jireh, Rapha, Nissi, and others), and the New Testament Greek names that carry these Hebrew concepts into the life of the Church.

The Three Layers of Divine Names

  • Foundational Names — El, Elohim, Yahweh (YHWH), Adonai, Ehyeh
  • Compound El-Names — El Shaddai, El Elyon, El Roi, El Olam, El Gibbor
  • Compound Yahweh-Names — Jireh, Rapha, Nissi, Shalom, Tsidkenu, Shammah, and others
  • New Testament Names — Theos, Kyrios, Abba, Pantokrator, Logos

Key Interpretive Principles

  • Each name emerges from a specific narrative context — find the story
  • Hebrew names are relational: God reveals who He is to someone
  • YHWH is the only proper personal name; all others are titles or descriptors
  • The plural Elohim with singular verbs anticipates Trinitarian understanding
  • New Testament Kyrios applied to Jesus is an explicit divine claim

Frequency in the Hebrew Bible

  • YHWH — 6,828 occurrences (Masoretic text)
  • Elohim — 2,500+ occurrences
  • Adon / Adonai — 434 occurrences
  • El Shaddai — ~48 occurrences (primarily Job, Pentateuch)
  • El Elyon — 28 occurrences (19 in Psalms)
  • El Roi — 1 occurrence (Genesis 16:13 — uniquely from Hagar)

On the Name "Jehovah"

  • Jehovah is not a Hebrew word — it emerged around the 16th century CE
  • Medieval scribes inserted Adonai's vowels into the YHWH consonants as a reading note
  • Non-Jewish readers mistakenly read them as one word: "Jehovah"
  • Scholars now prefer Yahweh (confirmed by early church writers like Clement of Alexandria)
  • English Bibles render YHWH as LORD (small caps) following the Septuagint's use of Kyrios

Foundational · Hebrew
אֱלֹהִים
Elohim
"God" — the Strong Creator
First use: Genesis 1:1
The very first word used for God in Scripture. Elohim opens the Bible — "In the beginning, Elohim created…" — and sets the tone: this is a God of power, creation, and sovereign authority over all things.
Etymology & Grammar
Elohim is grammatically plural (the -im suffix marks Hebrew masculine plural), yet consistently takes singular verbs when referring to the God of Israel. This grammatical tension is theologically significant — it has been interpreted variously as a "plural of majesty," a remnant of ancient Semitic usage, or (in Christian theology) an anticipation of Trinitarian plurality.
"In the beginning, God [Elohim] created the heavens and the earth." Genesis 1:1 (ESV)
Theological Significance
The root El carries the meaning of strength or power. Elohim is the infinite, all-powerful Creator, sustainer, and supreme judge. It is the cosmic name — God in relation to all of creation. GotQuestions notes: "Elohim is the infinite, all-powerful God who shows by His works that He is the creator, sustainer, and supreme judge of the world."
Contrast with Yahweh
In Genesis, Elohim creates (ch. 1); when the account turns personal in ch. 2, the name shifts to "Yahweh Elohim" (LORD God). This literary pattern suggests Elohim emphasizes cosmic power, while Yahweh emphasizes covenant relationship. The BibleProject observes: "In English and in Hebrew, the word 'God' (or El, or Elohim) is not a name, but a generic title for a deity."
Sources: GotQuestions.org; BibleProject, "God Has a Name"; Blue Letter Bible, Names of God; BiblicalHebrew.org
Foundational · The Personal Name
יהוה
Yahweh (YHWH)
"I Am Who I Am" — the Self-Existent One
First use: Genesis 2:4 · Revealed: Exodus 3:14–15
The Tetragrammaton — four consonants: Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh. The only proper, personal name of the God of Israel. Appears 6,828 times in the Hebrew Bible. Rendered LORD (small caps) in most English translations.
The Burning Bush Revelation
When Moses asks God His name at the burning bush, God replies: "Ehyeh asher Ehyeh" — "I Am Who I Am" (or "I Will Be What I Will Be"). He then instructs Moses to say "I AM has sent me." The third-person form of this verb is Yahweh — meaning "He Is" or "He Who Causes to Exist." The name encapsulates God's eternal self-existence and His faithfulness to covenant.
"God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And he said, 'Say this to the people of Israel: I AM has sent me to you.'" Exodus 3:14 (ESV)
Etymology
YHWH derives from the Hebrew root להיות (lihiot, "to be"). Scholars Anne Marie Kitz and others argue it means "to be," "to exist," "to bring into being." Christianity.com notes it can be interpreted as "He who will be, is, and has been" — a multiplicity of meanings that together point to God's eternal, unchanging nature.
Why "Jehovah" Is Inaccurate
Hebrew originally had no written vowels. Medieval Jewish scribes added vowel notation but replaced YHWH's vowels with those of Adonai as a reading reminder (never pronounce the sacred name — say Adonai instead). Non-Jewish scholars in the 16th century mistakenly merged the two, creating "Jehovah" — a word that never existed in ancient Hebrew. Scholars now widely prefer "Yahweh," attested as early as Clement of Alexandria (2nd century).
Jewish Tradition of Non-Pronunciation
After the Babylonian exile, Judaism progressively restricted use of the name until it was prohibited entirely. HaShem ("the Name") or Adonai was substituted. The reverence reflects a conviction that the name embodies God's very being — to pronounce it carelessly would be to treat the infinite casually. The Third Commandment (Exodus 20:7) forms the scriptural basis.
Sources: Christianity.com, "Tetragrammaton"; BartEhrman.com; CompellingTruth.org; BiblicalHebrew.org; Way.org.il Hebrew Names of God
Foundational · Hebrew
אֲדֹנָי
Adonai
"My Lord" — Sovereign Master
Root form: Genesis 15:2 · 434 occurrences
From Adon ("lord" or "master"), the plural form Adonai means literally "my lords" — used as a divine plural of majesty. Expresses God's sovereign authority and lordship over all creation and covenant people.
First Appearance in Context
The first notable use is Abraham's cry in Genesis 15:2: "O Lord GOD (Adonai Yahweh), what can You give me, since I remain childless?" The pairing of Adonai with Yahweh is theologically rich — the intimate Master and the covenant-keeping God addressed together in a moment of desperate trust.
"But Abram replied, 'O Sovereign LORD (Adonai Yahweh), what can you give me since I remain childless...?'" Genesis 15:2 (NIV)
Relationship to YHWH
Because YHWH was too sacred to pronounce, Adonai became its spoken substitute in Jewish worship. When a reader encountered the Tetragrammaton in the text, they would say "Adonai." This practice explains why English Bibles typically write "the LORD" (YHWH in Hebrew) and "the Lord" (Adonai in Hebrew) — a distinction visible in most printed Bibles.
Theological Significance
Blue Letter Bible notes: "Adonai occurs 434 times in the OT. It denotes exaltation and speaks of absolute right to lordship." Isaiah 6:8 uses "Adonai" — "I heard the voice of the Lord (Adonai) saying, 'Whom shall I send?'" — placing the word in a throne-room context that underscores God's sovereign majesty.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible, Names of God; Way.org.il; Christianity.com; GotQuestions.org
Foundational · Hebrew
אֶהְיֶה
Ehyeh
"I Will Be" — the Self-Declared Existence
Exodus 3:14 only
The first-person declaration God makes of Himself at the burning bush. "Ehyeh asher Ehyeh" — "I AM WHO I AM" or "I WILL BE WHAT I WILL BE." The basis from which Yahweh (third-person form) derives.
The Grammatical Relationship
Ehyeh is the first-person imperfect of the Hebrew verb "to be." Yahweh is the third-person form of the same verb. So when God says "I AM" (Ehyeh), He is telling Moses His own self-identification; when He instructs Moses to tell others "Yahweh sent me," He is giving the name by which the community can speak of Him. This is why Christianity.com notes: "Yahweh is the third-person version of Ehyeh."
"God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM (Ehyeh asher Ehyeh).' And he said, 'Say this to the people of Israel: I AM has sent me to you.'" Exodus 3:14 (ESV)
Augustine and Aquinas
Both Augustine of Hippo and Thomas Aquinas derived a metaphysical interpretation from this passage: God is Being itself — not a being among other beings, but the ground of all existence. Aquinas called God ipsum esse subsistens ("subsistent being itself"). This philosophical tradition flows directly from the Hebrew grammar of Ehyeh.
Jesus and the "I Am" Sayings
John's Gospel contains seven "I Am" (ἐγώ εἰμι) declarations by Jesus: Bread of Life, Light of the World, Good Shepherd, Gate, Resurrection, Way/Truth/Life, True Vine. In John 8:58, Jesus says "Before Abraham was, I Am" — using the absolute form that points directly back to Exodus 3:14. The crowd's attempt to stone Him shows they understood the claim perfectly.
Sources: Christianity.com; BartEhrman.com; GotQuestions.org; Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I.13
El Compound · Hebrew
אֵל שַׁדַּי
El Shaddai
"God Almighty" / "All-Sufficient God"
First use: Genesis 17:1 · ~48 occurrences
The name God uses when making His covenant promises to Abraham and the patriarchs. According to Exodus 6:3, this is the name by which Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob primarily knew God — before Yahweh was more fully revealed.
Etymology of Shaddai
The derivation of "Shaddai" is debated. Common proposals: (1) from the Hebrew shadad, "to be strong/powerful"; (2) from the Akkadian shadu, "mountain" — God as immovable, enduring; (3) from the Hebrew shad, "breast" — God as nourishing and sufficient provider. The Wikipedia article on El Shaddai notes the Arabic shadid (strong) as a cognate, though the phonetics are not a perfect match. The plurality suffix -ai functions as a pluralis excellentiae, like Elohim and Adonai.
"When Abram was ninety-nine years old, the LORD appeared to him and said, 'I am El-Shaddai — God Almighty. Serve me faithfully and live a blameless life.'" Genesis 17:1 (NLT)
The Patriarchal Name
Exodus 6:2–3 is a key text: "God spoke to Moses and said to him, 'I am Yahweh. I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob as El Shaddai, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them.'" This does not mean the patriarchs never heard the word Yahweh — it means that the full covenantal significance of that name was revealed progressively. El Shaddai was sufficient for the promise stage; Yahweh would be displayed through the Exodus.
Appearance in Job
The book of Job uses Shaddai 31 times — more than any other book. Job cries out to Shaddai in his suffering, the all-powerful God who seems absent. The name choice is deliberate: Job is wrestling with the God who has the power to both afflict and restore. The resolution comes when God answers from the whirlwind — as El Shaddai reasserting His sovereign sufficiency.
Sources: Christianity.com; Wikipedia (El Shaddai); Blue Letter Bible; RadiantPearlLiving.com
El Compound · Hebrew
אֵל עֶלְיוֹן
El Elyon
"God Most High"
First use: Genesis 14:18 · 28 OT occurrences
The name used by Melchizedek when blessing Abram after the battle of the kings. El Elyon emphasizes God's supreme sovereignty over all other rulers, nations, and spiritual powers. Appears 19 times in Psalms alone.
Melchizedek and the Name
Melchizedek, priest-king of Salem, blesses Abram: "Blessed be Abram by El Elyon, Creator of heaven and earth." Abram then uses the same name in swearing to the king of Sodom — identifying the God of Israel with the El Elyon of Melchizedek. This encounter is theologically rich: Hebrews 7 interprets Melchizedek as a type of Christ's eternal priesthood.
"And Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine. (He was priest of God Most High [El Elyon].) And he blessed him..." Genesis 14:18–19 (ESV)
Meaning of Elyon
Blue Letter Bible: "Elyon literally means 'Most High' and is used both adjectivally and substantivally throughout the OT. It expresses the extreme sovereignty and majesty of God and His highest preeminence." The combination El Elyon can be translated "the most exalted God" (Psalm 57:2).
NT Connection
The angel Gabriel tells Mary: "He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High (Hypsistos — Greek for Elyon)" (Luke 1:32). The New Testament deliberately draws on El Elyon language to assert that Jesus occupies the position of the Highest.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible; GotQuestions.org; BibleProject; Gospel Coalition, "Names of God"
El Compound · Hebrew
אֵל רֳאִי
El Roi
"The God Who Sees Me"
Only occurrence: Genesis 16:13
The most personal divine name in the Bible — given not by a patriarch or prophet, but by Hagar, an Egyptian slave woman, alone and desperate in the wilderness. She is the only person in Scripture to give God a new name.
Hagar's Story
Hagar had been given to Abram by Sarai as a surrogate. When she became pregnant, tension arose and Sarai mistreated her, causing her to flee into the wilderness. There, the Angel of the LORD found her by a spring — and spoke to her with compassion and promise. In that encounter, Hagar names God El Roi: "You are the God who sees me." She also wonders: "Have I truly seen the One who sees me?"
"Thereafter, Hagar used another name to refer to the LORD, who had spoken to her. She said, 'You are the God who sees me (El Roi).' She also said, 'Have I truly seen the One who sees me?'" Genesis 16:13 (NLT)
Theological Significance
This name is revolutionary in its context. Hagar is not a Hebrew. She is a slave, a woman, without social standing — and God appears specifically to her. The name she gives God stands as a permanent testimony: God sees those whom human society ignores. GotQuestions notes: "When Hagar met the Angel of the Lord, she realized she had seen God Himself in a theophany."
The Well's Name
The well where God appeared was named Beer-lahai-roi — "the well of the Living One who sees me." It appears again in Genesis 24:62 and 25:11 — Isaac lived near it. The physical location became a monument to the theological truth that God notices the overlooked.
Sources: GotQuestions.org; ScripturalGrace.com; Blue Letter Bible; Genesis 16 narrative
El Compound · Hebrew
אֵל עוֹלָם
El Olam
"The Everlasting God"
First use: Genesis 21:33 · Isaiah 40:28
Abraham calls on El Olam at Beersheba after his covenant with Abimelech. The name expresses God's eternal nature — He exists from everlasting to everlasting, unbound by time, never weary, never changing.
Abraham's Use
After settling a dispute over a well at Beersheba and planting a tamarisk tree, Abraham "called there on the name of the LORD, El Olam" (Genesis 21:33). The act of planting a long-lived tree at the site of calling on the Everlasting God is poetically intentional — yet even the tamarisk will die; El Olam will not.
"Have you not known? Have you not heard? The LORD is the everlasting God [El Olam], the Creator of the ends of the earth. He does not faint or grow weary..." Isaiah 40:28 (ESV)
Isaiah 40 and Renewal
Isaiah 40:28–31 is the great El Olam passage — written to exiles in Babylon who feared God had abandoned them. The prophet answers their despair by anchoring hope in God's eternal, unwearying nature: "He gives strength to the weary and increases the power of the weak." The everlastingness of God is the source of human renewal.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible; GotQuestions.org; Isaiah 40 commentary; Genesis 21 narrative
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יהוה יִרְאֶה
Yahweh Yireh (Jehovah Jireh)
"The LORD Will Provide" / "The LORD Who Sees to It"
Only occurrence: Genesis 22:14
Named by Abraham on Mount Moriah after God provided a ram to replace Isaac on the altar. The literal Hebrew is "Yahweh Will See" — to see, in Hebrew, carries the connotation of providing. God sees the need and provides for it.
The Sacrifice of Isaac
God commands Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac — the child of promise. Abraham obeys, and at the moment the knife is raised, God stops him: "Now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son, your only son, from me." A ram caught in a thicket is provided. Abraham names the place Yahweh Yireh.
"Abraham called the name of that place, 'The LORD will provide (Yahweh Yireh)'; as it is said to this day, 'On the mount of the LORD it shall be provided.'" Genesis 22:14 (ESV)
Bible.org Commentary
"The meaning of this name is The Lord Who Provides... This is what we long for when we have a need that is personal and special; One who will see to our needs and provide for us. One Who knows my need because He sees. One Who is able to meet my need in just the right time." — Bible.org, "The Compound Names of Jehovah"
Typology: The Ram and the Cross
Christian typology reads the ram as a foreshadowing of Christ — the substitutionary sacrifice provided by God. John 1:29 calls Jesus "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world." On "the mount of the LORD" (Moriah = Jerusalem), the ultimate provision was made — God providing the sacrifice Himself, as Abraham's words unwittingly prophesied: "God will provide for himself the lamb."
Sources: Bible.org; GospelMount.org; JesusPlusNothing.com; Genesis 22 narrative
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יהוה רֹפְאֶךָ
Yahweh Rapha (Jehovah Rapha)
"The LORD Who Heals You"
Only occurrence: Exodus 15:26
Revealed immediately after the Exodus, when Israel arrives at bitter waters and God makes them sweet. God's promise: "I am Yahweh your Healer" — a commitment that applies to physical, moral, and spiritual restoration.
Marah — Waters Made Sweet
Three days after crossing the Red Sea, Israel finds only bitter water at Marah. They grumble. God instructs Moses to throw a piece of wood into the water — it becomes sweet. The healing of the water is the sign; the name is the promise: "I am the LORD your Healer." The sequence of Jireh → Rapha in Exodus shows God revealing Himself progressively as needs arise.
"He said, 'If you will diligently listen to the voice of the LORD your God... I will put none of the diseases on you that I put on the Egyptians, for I am the LORD, your healer (Yahweh Rapha).'" Exodus 15:26 (ESV)
Scope of Rapha
The Hebrew root rapha covers physical healing (2 Kings 20:5), emotional healing (Psalm 147:3, "He heals the brokenhearted"), and spiritual/moral restoration (Hosea 14:4, "I will heal their waywardness"). Yahweh Rapha is not limited to medical recovery — it encompasses the full restoration of a broken life.
Isaiah 53 and Jesus the Healer
Isaiah 53:5 — "by his wounds we are healed" — is the Servant Song fulfillment of Yahweh Rapha. Matthew 8:17 quotes Isaiah 53 directly in connection with Jesus' healing ministry: "He took our infirmities and bore our diseases." The New Testament presents Jesus as the embodiment of Yahweh Rapha in human flesh.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible; GotQuestions.org; JesusPlusNothing.com; Isaiah 53 and Matt 8:17
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יהוה נִסִּי
Yahweh Nissi (Jehovah Nissi)
"The LORD Is My Banner"
Only occurrence: Exodus 17:15
Moses names an altar after the defeat of Amalek. While Joshua fought in the valley, Moses held his arms raised — and Israel prevailed only as long as his hands were lifted. The raised staff was the banner (nes) over the battle — and the battle belonged to God.
The Battle of Rephidim
Israel's first military engagement after the Exodus. Amalek attacks. Joshua commands the army below; Moses stands on a hill with the staff of God. When his hands are raised, Israel wins; when they drop from fatigue, Amalek advances. Aaron and Hur hold up Moses' arms until sunset — and Joshua defeats Amalek.
"Moses built an altar and called the name of it, The LORD Is My Banner (Yahweh Nissi), saying, 'A hand upon the throne of the LORD! The LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation.'" Exodus 17:15–16 (ESV)
Meaning of Nissi / Nes
The word nes refers to something lifted up — a pole, a standard, a banner used in ancient Near Eastern warfare to signal a rallying point. LogosApostolic.org notes it appears for the bronze serpent on a pole (Numbers 21:8–9), which Jesus explicitly connects to the cross in John 3:14: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up." Yahweh Nissi anticipates both the standard of the cross and the rallying of all nations to Christ.
Sources: LogosApostolic.org; Bible.org; JesusPlusNothing.com; GotQuestions.org
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יהוה שָׁלוֹם
Yahweh Shalom (Jehovah Shalom)
"The LORD Is Peace"
Only occurrence: Judges 6:24
Gideon names an altar after God reassures him he will not die from encountering the Angel of the LORD. The name "The LORD Is Peace" emerges from a moment of terror — and becomes a permanent theological statement.
Gideon's Fear
Gideon is threshing wheat in a winepress — hiding from the Midianites — when the Angel of the LORD appears and calls him a "mighty warrior." After a sign that consumes his offering with fire, Gideon realizes he has seen God and is terrified: "I have seen the Angel of the LORD face to face!" God says: "Peace be to you. Do not fear; you shall not die." Gideon builds an altar and names it Yahweh Shalom.
"Then Gideon built an altar there to the LORD and called it, The LORD Is Peace (Yahweh Shalom). To this day it still stands at Ophrah..." Judges 6:24 (ESV)
Shalom as More Than Peace
The Hebrew shalom is far richer than "absence of conflict." It carries the meaning of wholeness, completeness, welfare, health, and flourishing. Yahweh Shalom is not merely God who stops wars — He is God who restores everything broken, the author of complete human flourishing. Isaiah's messianic title "Prince of Peace" (Sar Shalom, Isaiah 9:6) names the one who will fully embody Yahweh Shalom.
Sources: GotQuestions.org; Blue Letter Bible; GospelMount.org; Judges 6 narrative
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יְהוָה צִדְקֵנוּ
Yahweh Tsidkenu (Jehovah Tsidkenu)
"The LORD Our Righteousness"
Jeremiah 23:6 · Jeremiah 33:16
A prophetic name given to the coming Messianic king — a ruler from David's line who will himself be the righteousness of His people. The name points forward to imputed righteousness in Christ.
The Messianic Context
Jeremiah 23:5–6 contrasts the corrupt kings of Judah (particularly the disastrous Zedekiah, whose name ironically means "Yahweh is righteous") with the coming Branch of David: "Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch... In his days Judah will be saved and Israel will dwell securely. And this is the name by which he will be called: 'The LORD is our righteousness (Yahweh Tsidkenu).'"
"...and this is the name by which he will be called: 'The LORD is our righteousness (Yahweh Tsidkenu).'" Jeremiah 23:6 (ESV)
Paul's Theology of Imputed Righteousness
NewCREEations notes: "God himself stands for us and provides us with His righteousness and justice when we don't have any in ourselves." This is Paul's central argument in Romans 3–5: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Yahweh Tsidkenu is the Old Testament anticipation of justification by faith.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible; GotQuestions.org; NewCREEations.org; GospelMount.org; Romans 3–5; 2 Cor 5:21
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יְהוָה שָׁמָּה
Yahweh Shammah (Jehovah Shammah)
"The LORD Is There"
Only occurrence: Ezekiel 48:35 (last verse of Ezekiel)
The final, climactic word of Ezekiel's vision of the restored city — the last three words of the entire book. After visions of departure and destruction, God's permanent presence returns. The name is the ultimate promise of Scripture: God with us.
Ezekiel's Journey to This Name
Ezekiel chapters 8–11 describe the glory of God departing from the Temple in stages — one of the most devastating passages in the OT. The presence that was Israel's greatest treasure withdraws because of their idolatry. The entire book then moves toward restoration, culminating in a vision of a new Temple (chs. 40–48) where God's glory returns (43:1–5). The book ends: "And the name of the city from that time on shall be, The LORD Is There."
"The circumference of the city shall be 18,000 cubits. And the name of the city from that time on shall be, The LORD Is There (Yahweh Shammah)." Ezekiel 48:35 (ESV)
New Testament Fulfillment
The Gospel of John opens: "The Word became flesh and dwelt (eskēnōsen — 'tabernacled') among us." John uses Temple/Tabernacle language to announce that Yahweh Shammah has arrived in Jesus. Revelation 21:3 closes the entire canon with the same promise: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God." Yahweh Shammah is the arc of the entire biblical story.
Sources: Wikipedia (Jehovah-shammah); NewCREEations.org; Blue Letter Bible; John 1:14; Revelation 21:3
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יהוה צְבָאוֹת
Yahweh Sabaoth (Lord of Hosts)
"LORD of Armies / LORD of Hosts"
First use: 1 Samuel 1:3 · ~285 OT occurrences
One of the most frequently used divine names in the prophets. Sabaoth means "armies" or "hosts" — referring to Israel's armies, the heavenly bodies, or angelic armies. A name of God's sovereign power over all cosmic and earthly forces.
Prophetic Weight
Isaiah uses Yahweh Sabaoth more than 60 times. It appears in the context of God's holiness (Isaiah 6:3 — "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts"), His judgment on nations, and His ultimate sovereignty. The name is a war title — it names God as the Commander of all forces, divine and human.
"And one called to another and said: 'Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts [Yahweh Sabaoth]; the whole earth is full of his glory!'" Isaiah 6:3 (ESV)
David and Sabaoth
David's challenge to Goliath uses this name: "You come to me with a sword and with a spear and with a javelin, but I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts (Yahweh Sabaoth), the God of the armies of Israel" (1 Samuel 17:45). The name is the basis of David's confidence — God commands armies Goliath cannot see.
NT Occurrence
Yahweh Sabaoth is transliterated directly into Greek as Sabaoth in Romans 9:29 (quoting Isaiah 1:9) and James 5:4. It is also rendered Kyrios Pantokrator ("Lord Almighty") in the NT — a title that appears 9 times in Revelation, emphasizing the sovereignty of God over all history.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible; Gospel Coalition; GotQuestions.org; 1 Samuel 17:45; Isaiah 6; Revelation
New Testament · Greek
Θεός · Κύριος
Theos · Kyrios
"God" · "Lord"
Theos: 1,318 NT uses · Kyrios: 700+ of Jesus
The two dominant NT divine names. Theos carries the weight of Elohim; Kyrios carries the weight of YHWH/Adonai. The application of Kyrios to Jesus — the same word used for YHWH in the Greek OT — is a central Christological claim.
Theos — Elohim in Greek
The Septuagint (Greek OT) translated Elohim as Theos. So when NT authors use Theos — 1,318 times — they are drawing on the full semantic weight of Elohim: the all-powerful Creator and sovereign God of Israel. BartEhrman.com notes: "The NT authors most often used this term to refer to God the Father."
"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God [Theos], and the Word was God [Theos]." John 1:1 (ESV)
Kyrios — YHWH Applied to Jesus
The Gospel Coalition: "YHWH came into the NT as kyrios, where it is used of God the Father about 100 times. But it is also used, over 700 times, with reference to Jesus." When Thomas declares "My Lord (Kyrios) and my God (Theos)" (John 20:28), he uses the two highest divine titles together for Jesus. Paul's confession "Jesus is Lord (Kyrios)" in Romans 10:9 uses the word that in the Septuagint stood for YHWH — an unmistakable divine identification.
The Christological Significance
Edge Induced Cohesion (2025) notes: "When Thomas declares 'My Lord and my God' (John 20:28)... he employs Greek terms to express the highest possible recognition of divinity. The confession 'Jesus is Lord' (Romans 10:9) uses the same Greek word that represented YHWH in Hebrew scripture, indicating the apostolic understanding of Jesus' divine identity."
Sources: Gospel Coalition, "Names of God"; BartEhrman.com; EdgeInducedCohesion.blog; John 1:1; John 20:28; Romans 10:9
New Testament · Aramaic / Greek
אַבָּא · Πατήρ
Abba · Pater
"Father" — intimate, familial address
Mark 14:36 · Romans 8:15 · Galatians 4:6
The most intimate divine address in Scripture. Jesus' preferred way of addressing God — and the address He teaches His disciples. "Abba" is the Aramaic word for father, deeply personal. Its appearance in Paul's letters signals that adopted children share the Son's access to the Father.
Jesus in Gethsemane
In his most agonizing hour, Jesus cries: "Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36). This is the only place in the Gospels where Abba appears directly from Jesus — the Aramaic word preserved by Mark alongside the Greek translation, underlining its weight and intimacy. In the horror of Gethsemane, Jesus still calls God "Father."
"For you did not receive the spirit of slavery to fall back into fear, but you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" Romans 8:15 (ESV)
Adoption and Access
Paul uses Abba twice (Romans 8:15; Galatians 4:6), both times in the context of adoption. The Spirit given to believers is not a spirit of fear but of sonship — and the evidence is this cry: Abba. The name signals that through Christ, believers receive the same relational access to the Father that the Son has. BibleProject notes: "Pater or Father seems to be Jesus' preferred designation for God."
Revolution in Addressing God
In the OT, God is occasionally called Father (Isaiah 63:16; 64:8), but it is rare and always corporate. Jesus makes it the normative address — "Our Father" (Matthew 6:9). The intimacy of Abba was unprecedented in Second Temple Judaism and marks one of the most distinctive features of Jesus' teaching about God.
Sources: Religion.fandom.com; BibleProject; GotQuestions.org; Mark 14:36; Romans 8:15; Gal 4:6
New Testament · Greek
Λόγος
Logos
"The Word" — the Living, Creative Self-Expression of God
John 1:1–14 · Revelation 19:13
John's prologue identifies Jesus as the Logos — God's eternal Word, the agent of creation, the divine self-expression who became flesh. The term bridges Hebrew "Wisdom" literature, the Genesis creation narrative, and Greek philosophical usage.
Hebrew Background: Dabar
In Hebrew thought, God's dabar (word) is not merely speech — it is creative, effective, powerful. "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made" (Psalm 33:6). Proverbs 8 personifies Wisdom as present with God at creation. John identifies Jesus as this personal divine Word — the one through whom "all things were made" (John 1:3).
"In the beginning was the Word [Logos], and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory..." John 1:1, 14 (ESV)
Greek Philosophical Context
In Greek philosophy (especially Stoicism), Logos was the rational principle governing the cosmos. Philo of Alexandria used Logos as God's intermediary in creation. John takes this culturally familiar term and fills it with explosive new content: the Logos is not an abstract principle — He is a person who can be seen, touched, and known (1 John 1:1–3). The Word became flesh — divine reason became a human being.
Revelation 19:13
The final use of Logos as a title for Jesus appears in Revelation's vision of the returning King: "He is clothed in a robe dipped in blood, and the name by which he is called is The Word of God (Logos tou Theou)." Alpha and Omega, Faithful and True, King of kings, Lord of lords — and, finally, the Word of God. The name that opens John's Gospel closes the scriptural canon.
Sources: Gospel Coalition; BibleProject; John 1:1–14; Revelation 19:13; Philo of Alexandria, On the Creation
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יְהוָה רֹעִי
Yahweh Raah / Yahweh Ro'i (Jehovah Raah)
"The LORD Is My Shepherd" / "The LORD My Friend"
Primary use: Psalm 23:1 · Also: Genesis 48:15; 49:24; Psalm 80:1
The most beloved divine name in the Psalter — and one of the most intimate in all of Scripture. David, himself a former shepherd, uses his own vocation as the lens through which he describes God's personal care, provision, and companionship. The root word for shepherd also carries the meaning of "companion" or "close friend."
Etymology — Raah / Ro'i
The Hebrew root ra'ah means "to pasture," "to tend," or "to care for flocks." As a verb it carries the sense of feeding and leading; as a noun (ro'i / rohi) it means "shepherd" or "pasture." Blue Letter Bible notes a critical extended meaning: the related word rea' means "friend" or "companion." So Yahweh Raah can be rendered not only "The LORD my Shepherd" but also, theologically, "The LORD my Friend" — pointing to the intimacy God desires with His people, not merely His function as provider.
"The LORD is my shepherd (Yahweh Ro'i); I shall not want. He makes me lie down in green pastures. He leads me beside still waters. He restores my soul." Psalm 23:1–3 (ESV)
David's Biographical Context
ONE FOR ISRAEL Ministry notes that Psalm 23 was written by "King David, who changed career from being a shepherd to a king, with a good few years as a wandering fugitive in between." David was a hunted man for years under Saul — ducking through the Judean wilderness. When he writes "though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil," it is not poetry at a safe remove. It is the testimony of a man who lived it. The word "my" in "my shepherd" is possessive and personal — David is not describing God as the shepherd of Israel in the abstract, but as his own shepherd in the specific.
Jacob's Earlier Use
The shepherd image precedes Psalm 23. Jacob blesses Joseph's sons and refers to "the God who has been my shepherd all my life long to this day" (Genesis 48:15) — using the same ra'ah root. Genesis 49:24 calls God "the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel." The name's narrative roots go deep into the patriarchal era, though its definitive lyrical expression is Psalm 23.
Jesus as the Fulfillment
John 10:11 — "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" — is Jesus' direct claim to be Yahweh Raah in human flesh. Every element of Psalm 23 finds its fullest expression in Christ: green pastures (John 6:35, Bread of Life), still waters (John 4:14, living water), the valley of death (John 11:25, resurrection), the table before enemies (the Lord's Supper), dwelling in the house of the LORD (John 14:2–3). Hebrews 13:20 calls Jesus "the great Shepherd of the sheep."
Sources: Blue Letter Bible BLB Blog; BibleInfo.com; ONE FOR ISRAEL Ministry; Christianity.com (Jehovah Rohi); ScripturalGrace.com; GatewayEPC.org
Yahweh Compound · Hebrew
יְהוָה מְקַדִּשְׁכֶם
Yahweh Mekoddishkem (Jehovah M'Kaddesh)
"The LORD Who Sanctifies You" / "The LORD Who Sets You Apart"
Two occurrences: Exodus 31:13 · Leviticus 20:8
One of only two compound Yahweh-names that God declares of Himself — not named by a human being in response to an encounter, but spoken directly by God as a self-identification. Given in the context of Sabbath law, it carries the full weight of Israel's distinct calling: a holy God makes a holy people.
Etymology — Mekoddishkem / Qadash
Mekoddishkem derives from the Hebrew root qadash, meaning "to sanctify," "to make holy," or "to set apart as dedicated." Blue Letter Bible defines it as "the separation of an object or person to the dedication of the Holy." The suffix -kem is second person plural — "you" (the people). So the name is both personal and communal: God sanctifies each individual within the covenant community. Variant spellings include Jehovah M'Kaddesh and Jehovah Mekadesh.
"You are to speak to the people of Israel and say, 'Above all you shall keep my Sabbaths, for this is a sign between me and you throughout your generations, that you may know that I, the LORD, sanctify you (Yahweh Mekoddishkem).'" Exodus 31:13 (ESV)
The Sabbath as the Sign
Both occurrences of this name appear in the context of Sabbath-keeping and holiness commands. This is not coincidental. The Sabbath was Israel's most visible covenant marker — the sign that distinguished them from surrounding nations. By connecting His sanctifying name to the Sabbath, God is saying: my act of setting you apart is demonstrated by your rest. Holiness is not merely ethical behavior — it is rest in what God has done. The Sabbath-as-sign anticipates the New Testament theology of rest in Christ (Hebrews 4:9–11).
The Tension: God's Work and Human Response
BibleInfo.com captures the essential tension precisely: "He says, 'I am the Lord who sanctifies you.' It is God who is doing the sanctifying, the making holy — not us." Yet both passages also include commands: "Consecrate yourselves" (Leviticus 20:7); "Keep My statutes and perform them" (v. 8). This is the same paradox Paul articulates in Philippians 2:12–13 — "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you." Mekoddishkem names the prior and primary work: God sanctifies. Human obedience is the response, not the cause.
New Testament Fulfillment
Jesus prays: "Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth" (John 17:17) — invoking the same divine sanctifying work on His disciples. 1 Corinthians 1:30 calls Christ "our righteousness and sanctification and redemption." Hebrews 10:10 states: "we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all." Yahweh Mekoddishkem is not an abstract OT category — it is the character of God that drives the atonement. God sets apart His Son to set apart His people.
Sources: Blue Letter Bible BLB Blog; BibleInfo.com; WhatThenWhyNow.org; FaithChapel.org; Exodus 31:13; Leviticus 20:7–8; John 17:17; 1 Cor 1:30; Hebrews 10:10

Theological Themes

The architecture of divine self-disclosure through naming

📖
Progressive Revelation
God reveals His names progressively as His relationship with His people deepens. The patriarchs knew El Shaddai; Moses received Yahweh; the prophets developed Tsidkenu and Shammah; the NT adds Abba and Logos. Each name is given at the point of need — God does not reveal more than His people can receive.
See: Exodus 6:2–3; Hebrews 1:1–2
🤝
Names Emerge from Narrative
Nearly every compound name comes from a specific crisis: Hagar in the desert (El Roi), Abraham on Moriah (Jireh), Moses at Marah (Rapha), Gideon hiding in a winepress (Shalom). God names Himself in relationship — not in abstraction. The names are always attached to a story of need met.
See: Gen 16:13; Gen 22:14; Exod 15:26; Judg 6:24
⚖️
The Name as Covenant
To know God's name is to stand in covenant with Him. Exodus 3 connects the revelation of YHWH directly to covenant promises ("I will bring you out... I will give you the land..."). The Third Commandment protects the name because it protects the covenant relationship — misusing the name is misusing the relationship.
See: Exodus 3:13–15; 20:7; Nehemiah 9:5
✝️
Christ as the Fullness of Every Name
Each divine name finds its fullest expression in Jesus: Jireh — the Lamb God provides (John 1:29); Rapha — healing by His wounds (Isaiah 53:5); Nissi — lifted up on the cross (John 3:14); Shalom — "he himself is our peace" (Ephesians 2:14); Tsidkenu — our righteousness in Christ (2 Cor 5:21); Shammah — "I am with you always" (Matthew 28:20).
See: Colossians 2:9; John 14:6–9; Revelation 22:13
🌍
The Singular Name
Despite the plurality of names, Scripture is insistent on the singularity of God. "Hear O Israel, the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). The many names are not many gods — they are one God seen from many angles, as light through a prism. Each name is a partial but true glimpse of an infinite Being.
See: Deuteronomy 6:4; Isaiah 45:5; 1 Corinthians 8:6
🏛️
Yahweh Shammah as the Arc of Scripture
The Bible begins: "In the beginning, God" — God present with creation. Eden breaks that presence. The entire narrative arc is the story of God working to restore His presence among His people — tabernacle, Temple, Jesus (Emmanuel, God-with-us), the Spirit's indwelling, and finally Revelation 21:3. Yahweh Shammah is the telos of Scripture.
See: Genesis 3; Ezekiel 48:35; John 1:14; Revelation 21:3
The Unifying Logic

BiblicalHebrew.org captures the principle well: these names "are not boundaries or categories, but revelations, helping finite beings relate to an infinite God." The names of God are not an exhaustive catalog of what God is — they are thresholds of encounter. Each name opens a door. Each name invites the worshipper not merely to learn about God, but to meet the God it describes.

"In the Old Testament times, a name was not only identification, but an identity as well." — Blue Letter Bible, Names of God

Study Questions

For personal devotion, small group, or sermon preparation

Observation · Exodus 3:14
God answers Moses' question "What is your name?" with "I AM WHO I AM" — not a name in the usual sense, but a statement of being. What does this response suggest about the nature of God and the limits of human language in describing Him?
Hint: Consider what a "name" is supposed to do, and why God's answer both answers and transcends the question.
Observation · Genesis 16:13
Hagar — a foreign slave woman — is the only person in Scripture to give God a new name. She calls Him El Roi: "The God who sees me." What does it say about God's character that this name comes from someone at the absolute bottom of ancient society's hierarchy?
Hint: Consider who else in the Bible encounters God in moments of social invisibility (Elijah under the broom tree, the disciples in the storm, Paul in prison).
Interpretation · Exodus 6:2–3
God tells Moses: "I appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as El Shaddai, but by my name Yahweh I did not make myself known to them." The patriarchs clearly used the word Yahweh (see Genesis 15:2). What does this statement mean about the progressive nature of divine revelation? What was "known" in Exodus that was not yet known to Abraham?
Hint: Think about the difference between knowing someone's name and knowing them fully — and what the Exodus would demonstrate about Yahweh that the patriarchs had not yet seen.
Synthesis · Compound Names Pattern
Nearly every compound Yahweh-name (Jireh, Rapha, Nissi, Shalom) emerges from a specific crisis narrative. What does this pattern tell you about when and why God chooses to reveal new aspects of His character? Do you think this pattern continues in your own experience?
Hint: Consider how none of these names were revealed in comfortable, peaceful settings.
Theology · Ezekiel 48:35
The entire book of Ezekiel moves from the departure of God's glory (chs. 8–11) to the name Yahweh Shammah — "The LORD Is There" (48:35). How does this narrative arc illuminate the nature of judgment and restoration? How does John 1:14 ("the Word became flesh and dwelt among us") function as a fulfillment of this movement?
Hint: The Greek word for "dwelt" in John 1:14 (eskēnōsen) is literally "tabernacled" — a direct Temple/Shekinah reference.
Application · Yahweh Tsidkenu (Jeremiah 23:6)
Yahweh Tsidkenu — "The LORD Our Righteousness" — is a name given to the coming Messiah in Jeremiah. Paul in 2 Corinthians 5:21 says believers "become the righteousness of God" in Christ. What are the practical implications of relating to God under this name? What changes when someone moves from "I must become righteous" to "I am given righteousness"?
Hint: Consider how this name functions differently for someone carrying guilt versus someone resting in justification.
NT Connection · Mark 14:36 / Romans 8:15
Jesus cries "Abba, Father" in Gethsemane — his most desperate moment. Paul says believers can use this same cry because of the Spirit of adoption. Why does the intimacy of "Abba" matter most at the point of suffering rather than at the point of comfort? What does it mean that God wants to be addressed this way?
Hint: The contrast with the OT's reverence for the unpronounced YHWH is stark — Jesus collapses that distance in one word.
Synthesis · The Whole Picture
If you had to select the three names of God that most powerfully speak to your current season of life, which would they be — and why? How might a regular practice of praying through the names of God change the character of your prayer life?
Hint: Consider Psalm 9:10 — "those who know your name put their trust in you." Knowing the name is the basis of trust.
