# Forbes Business Council — Authoring Guidance

This file is Harper's operating reference for all Forbes Business Council article submissions on David's behalf. Read this file in full before drafting or revising any Forbes article. It synthesizes the official Forbes submission guidelines with David's voice rules and the lessons learned from the first two submissions.

---

## The Non-Negotiables

These will get an article rejected or flagged without appeal.

**Word count: 500–1,000 words. Target 700–800.** The title does not count. Subheadings do not count. Body prose only. If you are over 1,000, cut. If you are under 500, you have not developed the argument.

**No AI-generated content.** Forbes uses AI detection software. The signals they scan for are the same habits that weak AI writing produces: bullet points in the body, bold keyword formatting, repetitive list structures, generic statements without firsthand grounding, and unverifiable claims. David's consulting vantage point is the antidote. Every paragraph should be anchored in something he has actually seen, heard, or lived. If a paragraph could have been written by someone who has never run a delivery team, rewrite it.

**Original and unpublished.** Nothing submitted to Forbes can have appeared anywhere else first — not on Driven to Develop, not on LinkedIn, not as a LinkedIn newsletter, not anywhere. Forbes holds exclusive rights for 30 days post-publication. Do not repurpose existing blog posts. Build new.

**Single byline.** David is the sole author. No co-authors, no co-bylines.

**No self-promotion in the body.** Improving does not get linked or named in the article body. The author bio handles attribution. The article body is pure argument.

**Citations required.** Every specific factual claim needs a hyperlinked citation to an authoritative, verifiable source published within the last two years. No vague attributions ("studies show," "research suggests"). If a claim cannot be sourced, rewrite it as David's direct observation: "In my work with clients..." or "Across the engagements my teams run..." That framing is honest, defensible, and sounds more like David anyway.

## Format Rules

**Prose only.** No bullet points anywhere in the body. No numbered lists. If you are tempted to list things, write them as a sentence: "There are three things worth tracking: decision quality, speed from insight to action, and team capability accumulation." That reads better and sounds more like David.

**No bold formatting in the body.** Bold keywords are an AI detection flag. No bold subheadings. No bolded terms mid-paragraph. Clean prose throughout.

**No markdown headers in the body (##, ###).** This is a submission form entry, not a structured document. Plain text from title to close.

**Title Case headlines.** The article title uses Title Case. Capitalize the major words.

**Structure: introduction, body, conclusion.** Forbes requires a clear business angle and a conclusion. This maps naturally to David's four-part arc (hook, story/observation, insight, challenge). The adjustment for Forbes is that the introduction needs to be more substantive than a single scene-setter — give the reader the business problem within the first two paragraphs.

**No third-party solicited quotes.** David's direct experience is the authority. Supporting citations link to studies and reports, not to interviews or quotes he arranged for the piece.

## David's Voice in a Forbes Context

David's differentiator at Forbes is the same as everywhere else: he runs delivery teams. His consultants are in client environments doing this work today. When he writes about AI adoption, it is not theoretical — someone on his team is managing that deployment right now. That proximity is what separates his articles from generic thought leadership, and it is exactly what Forbes is looking for when they ask for "firsthand expertise."

Apply the same voice rules that govern all of David's public writing:

- No em-dashes. Ever. Commas, periods, or parentheses.
- Parenthetical asides are natural and welcome: "(and I've seen this across geographies)" "(not the answer most boards want to hear)".
- Warmth is real. He writes to people, not at them.
- The closing challenge is his signature. It asks something of the reader. It is never a summary, never "I hope this resonates," never a call to follow or subscribe.

## Citation Workflow

Before finalizing any draft, identify every factual claim and ask: can I find a real, verifiable, recent source for this?

If yes: hyperlink it inline using markdown. Example: `[McKinsey's 2025 State of AI report](https://www.mckinsey.com/...)` finds that only one-third of organizations are scaling AI programs.

If no: rewrite the claim as David's direct observation. "In almost every engagement my teams run, there are two or three specific friction points blocking the majority of the team." That is honest, specific, and does not need a citation.

Good citation sources for David's typical topics:
- McKinsey Global Institute (State of AI reports, annual)
- Gartner (CFO/CIO advisory, AI ROI analysis)
- MIT Sloan Management Review (AI adoption and workforce)
- Harvard Business Review (leadership and organizational behavior)
- Writer.com enterprise AI adoption survey (workforce-level AI usage)
- Salesforce State of Data and AI reports

All sources must be from 2024 or later for current submissions.

## Pre-Submission Checklist

Run through this before every submission.

- [ ] Word count: 700–800 words (body only)
- [ ] No bold formatting anywhere in body
- [ ] No bullet points or numbered lists in body
- [ ] No markdown headers (##, ###) in body
- [ ] No em-dashes
- [ ] Title is Title Case
- [ ] Every factual claim has a hyperlinked citation OR is rewritten as first-person observation
- [ ] All citations link to sources from 2024 or later
- [ ] No mention of Improving or any company URL in the body
- [ ] Content has not been published anywhere else
- [ ] Closing paragraph is a challenge or question — not a summary, not "I hope this helps"
- [ ] Passes the "could only David write this" test — every paragraph is grounded in his direct experience

## File Naming Convention

`YYYY-MM-DD-slug.md` — date is the draft date, slug is 3-5 hyphenated lowercase words from the title.

Example: `2026-06-19-executive-ai-adoption-gap.md`

Save all Forbes article files to `content/forbes/`.
