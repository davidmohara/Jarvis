# Why Your Next Crisis Will Expose the Leader You've Actually Built

I was in a client briefing when the call came through. A critical system had gone down mid-deployment. Three hundred users offline. The clock running. And the most senior person in the room looked around the table as if someone else was supposed to speak first.

That pause lasted maybe four seconds. But in four seconds, I watched an entire room take its emotional cue from one person's hesitation. Anxiety is contagious. I've seen it spread faster than any system failure.

The leaders who maintain clarity when everyone else panics aren't superhuman. They've simply built systems that beat their own biology before the crisis arrives.

Shane Parrish, in "Clear Thinking," identifies four biological defaults that activate the moment pressure spikes. The emotion default floods your system and multiplies your progress by zero. The ego default shifts your priority from being right to feeling right, so you defend positions instead of seeking truth. The social default pulls you toward the room's anxiety rather than toward what the situation actually requires. The inertia default makes you cling to familiar approaches precisely when circumstances demand rapid adaptation. These aren't character flaws. Neuroscience is unambiguous on this: high levels of stress hormones physically shift your brain from the thoughtful prefrontal cortex to the reactive amygdala. You stop thinking. You start reacting.

The cruel irony is that these responses evolved to keep us alive. Our ancestors needed instant reaction when a predator appeared. They didn't need measured deliberation. But in a modern business crisis, those same instincts lead directly to the wrong moves.

Edwin Friedman's concept of the non-anxious presence offers the countermodel. Friedman argues that a leader's primary function in a crisis is not to solve the problem first. It is to regulate the emotional field so that the organization can think. When you maintain composure, you become a kind of immune system: filtering out the contagion, keeping energy focused on what actually matters. The paradox, and it took me a long time to believe this, is that calm leadership generates more useful urgency than panic does.

Geoffrey Toffetti experienced this firsthand. His hospitality company faced a 90% revenue collapse in March 2020. While competitors scrambled to salvage whatever revenue remained, Toffetti made a decision that looked counterintuitive from the outside: he paused all client agreements and focused entirely on preserving relationships. No panic-driven cuts. No bridge-burning. That choice, made from clarity rather than fear, became the company's inflection point. The loyalty he built during the collapse paid out when business returned. That is what Friedman means by taking maximum responsibility for your response rather than trying to control outcomes you cannot predict.

Here is what I know from watching delivery teams operate under pressure: your performance in a crisis is almost entirely determined by what you built in the months before it arrived. Research on Navy SEAL candidates has consistently found that those who held a "stress-is-enhancing" mindset showed greater persistence, faster obstacle course times, and better peer evaluations. They didn't experience less stress. They had built systems to channel it.

Emergency medicine figured this out before business did. ER physicians use a framework called "Breathe, Talk, See, Focus" to maintain clarity during resuscitations. The breathing anchor, box breathing, is four seconds in, four seconds hold, four seconds out, four seconds hold. You trace the sides of a square in your mind as you breathe. It measurably lowers heart rate and blood pressure, dampening the hormonal cascade that would otherwise impair your judgment. Navy SEALs use the same technique before high-stakes operations. I use it before difficult client conversations. The phrase they live by is worth keeping: slow is smooth, smooth is fast.

Three things are worth building before you need them. The first is a daily regulation practice, whether meditation, structured exercise, or quiet time before the calendar starts. In my experience managing distributed teams, leaders with high self-awareness consistently make better decisions under pressure, and self-awareness is not innate. It is practiced. The second is a pre-established decision framework, because when crisis hits you will not have the bandwidth to invent one. The pattern in SEAL performance research is consistent: the adaptive candidates were the ones who would "take extra time to get additional facts, and delay decision making rather than make an error." The third is psychological safety on your team. Harvard's Amy Edmondson has been direct on this for years: psychological safety is mission critical during crisis, not a luxury. Without it, people hide the information you need most precisely when you need it.

When the moment arrives, five moves matter: name which default is being triggered, activate what you have already practiced, narrow your focus to what you can actually control, communicate with the composure your team needs to mirror, and hold the strategic frame. The pattern from the 2008 crisis is well-documented: the companies that outperformed peers had made disciplined decisions before the downturn hit, not during it. Today's emergency can destroy tomorrow's position if you let it. Don't.

Here is the piece that most leadership frameworks leave out: calm is contagious, and so is panic. When you maintain clarity, you don't just make better decisions. You create the conditions for everyone around you to think. You become the steady point in a spinning room. [Sports psychology research](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2024.2414442) shows that athletes who perform in clutch moments use approach strategies rather than avoidance strategies. They channel the pressure rather than flee it. They stay present with the challenge rather than rushing to escape the discomfort. The same dynamic plays out in every leadership crisis I have watched up close.

That four-second pause I described at the beginning of this piece resolved itself. Someone spoke. The room oriented. The system came back up. But I have also been in rooms where the pause kept extending, where the senior person's anxiety set the emotional ceiling for everyone else, where the crisis became a rout not because of the technical failure but because of what the leader's face said before they said anything.

You do not know when your next crisis will arrive. But you can ask yourself right now: what is the daily practice you are actually running? What is the decision framework you would actually reach for? And when was the last time someone on your team told you something true that you did not want to hear?
