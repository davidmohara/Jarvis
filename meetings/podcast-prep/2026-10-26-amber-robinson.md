## Episode Topic

**"AI Meets the Law: In-House Counsel on Risk, Policy, and the New Legal Economics"** (title TBD, flagged in Janine's doc)

AI adoption has outrun the legal frameworks around it. Employees are pasting client data into public tools, contracts were not written for AI-generated deliverables, and nobody is quite sure who owns what AI produces. Meanwhile, AI is quietly changing the economics of legal work itself: tasks that billed ten hours now take one, which puts pressure on hourly billing and on the roles built around it.

Core themes:
- The real legal risks of employees using AI at work (data exposure, ownership, liability)
- What a minimum viable AI usage policy looks like, and what companies leave out
- Shadow AI: governing what employees actually do without creating a culture of fear
- The billing shift: outcome-based vs hourly, seen from the buyer's side of the table
- Why AI elevates paralegals and junior legal roles instead of eliminating them
- A working lawyer's honest take on AI: where it helps, where it creates new problems

**Segment 1: Emerging Legal Issues in the AI Space**

**1. [Janine Q1] The risks nobody budgeted for.** What are the biggest legal risks companies take on when their employees start using AI tools at work?
*→ Mechanism: What does that actually look like when it goes wrong? Give us the scenario that keeps a corporate counsel up at night.*

**2. [Janine Q4] The policy gap.** What should an AI usage policy cover at minimum, and which sections do companies most often leave out?
*→ Mechanism: You are literally writing ours right now. Where did you start, and what did you find yourself adding that was not in any template?*

**3. [Janine Q7] When AI gets it wrong.** If an AI tool gives a client wrong advice or a flawed deliverable, who is liable, and how do contracts typically allocate that risk?
*→ Devil's advocate: Let me push back. Every vendor contract we sign tries to push that risk back on us. Is liability allocation in AI contracts actually negotiable, or is that fiction for anyone but the biggest buyers?*

**4. [Janine Q9] Shadow AI.** How should a company handle "shadow AI," meaning employees using tools that IT never approved, without creating a culture of fear?
*→ Mechanism: How does that show up in practice? Are we talking about finding it through expense reports and browser logs, or is there a smarter way to surface it?*

**5. [Janine Q10] The first 90 days.** If you were advising a mid-sized company that is just starting its AI journey, what three things would you tell them to do in the first 90 days?

**Segment 2: The Billing Shift and Why Paralegals Aren't Going Anywhere** *(fresh material, framed for Amber as in-house counsel, a buyer of legal services, not a law-firm insider)*

**6. The view from the buyer's chair.** You sit on the buying side of legal services now. AI is compressing the hours behind legal work, research that took a junior associate ten hours takes one. Are the firms you work with actually moving off hourly billing toward outcome-based or flat-fee arrangements, or is that still mostly conference-panel talk?
*→ Devil's advocate: The billable hour has survived every technology wave for fifty years. Word processors, email, e-discovery, all of it. What would someone who disagrees say: why is AI different this time?*

**7. What you would pay for.** If a firm came to you with an outcome-based proposal instead of a rate card, what would you actually want to see in it to say yes? What does "outcome" even mean for legal work, and how do you price it?
*→ Mechanism: How would that change your relationship with outside counsel day to day? Fewer invoice fights, more strategy?*

**8. Why paralegals survive this.** There is a running prediction that AI eliminates paralegals and junior legal roles first. You work closest to where that work actually happens. Make the case for why paralegals do not disappear.
*→ Mechanism: What does the paralegal role look like in three years then? Which parts of the job does AI take, and which parts get more valuable?*

**Segment 3: A Working Lawyer's Honest Take on AI**

**9. [Janine Q12] The good and the bad.** What's a situation where AI made your job easier, and what's one where it created more problems than it solved?

**10. [Janine Q13] Caught off guard.** Has there been a moment when a client's AI question caught you off guard, and how did you handle it?

**11. [Janine Q14] Your own shift.** How has your own relationship with AI tools changed since you started working on these issues?

**12. [Janine Q15] The closer.** If there was one takeaway you would want listeners to have after today's episode, what would it be?

## David's Talking Points & Angles

- **Personal experience:** Improving is writing its own Employee AI Usage policy right now, with Amber, and David opened that thread personally in September. He can reference the real internal conversation: "We are going through this ourselves, literally with you." Also his own Jarvis/multi-agent build gives him a ground truth on what employees actually do with AI tools versus what policy assumes they do.
- **Improving angle:** Improving sells AI delivery to clients while simultaneously governing AI use internally. That dual position, vendor and governed enterprise, is exactly where Amber sits legally. David can frame Improving as a company eating its own cooking: AI usage policy, vendor terms review, client data boundaries.
- **Contrarian or fresh take:** Most "AI and the law" content comes from law-firm partners selling caution. Amber is in-house at a tech consultancy: she has to make AI usable, not just safe. The interesting tension is enablement vs lockdown, and David can push the idea that the best AI policy is the one employees actually follow.
- **Story to tell:** The shadow AI angle is personal. David runs agents and automations daily. He can admit on air that the tools people love spread faster than any approval process, and ask Amber how policy catches up to reality without killing the energy.
- **Key frame:** AI risk is not a reason to wait; it is a reason to write things down. The companies that win are the ones whose legal team is in the room early, as an enabler, and the legal profession itself is being repriced around outcomes rather than hours.
