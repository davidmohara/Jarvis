## 1. The Forward Deployed Model Changes Everything

**Tags:** AI, business, leadership
**Feature/Twitter image:** https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1758873268745-dd2cf0d677b5?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wxMjA3fDB8MXxhbGx8fHx8fHx8fHwxNzc5MDIxMDg2fA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=2000
**Meta/Twitter description:** Accenture and ServiceNow just named a delivery model consulting firms already use but never packaged. Here's why that matters now.

Accenture and ServiceNow didn't just sign a partnership this week. They gave a name to something I've watched work for years without a name: forward deployed engineering. Put the team inside the client's environment before the enterprise rollout even gets approved. Build it there. Prove it there. Then talk about scale.

I read the announcement twice because I kept waiting for the part where it sounded like a big-firm move, all slides and steering committees. It never came. What they described is closer to how the best work at Improving actually gets done: small teams, real proximity to the client's mess, something shipped before the contract gets bigger. We've just never called it that. We've never packaged it, priced it, or put it on a slide of its own.

That's the part that nags at me. The methodology is already ours in practice. What we're missing is the discipline to name it and sell it as the thing itself, rather than as a nice side effect of "how we happen to work."

Here's what I think this changes for consulting broadly. For years, the pitch was expertise: come see our AI transformation deck, our maturity model, our framework. That pitch is losing ground fast. The firms winning the next phase aren't the ones with the best slides. They're the ones who can show up in a client's actual environment and have something running in production by next quarter, not next year. That's not a staffing question. It's a methodology question, and most firms still answer it like a staffing question.

I've sat across the table from clients who've been burned by exactly that mismatch (a beautiful roadmap, a year of nothing shipped). Forward deployment is the antidote, and it's not new to us. It's just unclaimed.

So here's what I'm asking myself this week, and I'd ask you the same thing if you run a practice: what would a forward deployed version of your AI work actually look like, on paper, with a name and a price tag? Not as a thought exercise. Start drafting it this week.

## 2. Fort Worth Just Became a Tier-One AI City

**Tags:** AI, business, technology
**Feature/Twitter image:** https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1631662760570-8f7b52d09fdc?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wxMjA3fDB8MXxhbGx8fHx8fHx8fHwxNzc4MzE0MDAzfA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=2000
**Meta/Twitter description:** A $761M AI supercomputing facility just landed in Fort Worth. DFW isn't riding the AI wave anymore, it's building the infrastructure.

$761 million. 1.1 million square feet. 800 jobs. A Nvidia supplier just picked Fort Worth over every other city in the country to build its first U.S. AI supercomputing facilities, and I don't think enough people around here have clocked what that means yet.

I've been telling anyone who'll listen that DFW is turning into a real AI market, not just a place with a lot of AI talent, but a place where the physical infrastructure of AI is actually getting built. Wistron's AllianceTexas announcement isn't a corporate relocation story. It's the compute layer, the actual machinery enterprise AI runs on, going up twenty minutes from where most of my clients operate. Add that to the 248 data centers planned across the state and AI engineering topping the list of DFW's fastest-growing jobs, and the "emerging market" thesis I've been running with for a year is done. It arrived.

Here's what changes when your region stops riding the wave and starts being load-bearing infrastructure for it. The conversation with clients shifts. It used to be "should we take AI seriously." Now it's closer to "we're behind, and we didn't notice." I say that carefully, because I don't think most leaders here actually are behind. But the runway is shorter than it looks from a comfortable office in Uptown.

What I keep coming back to is proximity. When the infrastructure, the talent, and the client base are all in the same twenty-mile radius, the excuse of "we'll get to AI strategy next year" stops holding up. Somebody down the street is already building it.

If you're leading a business in this market and you haven't mapped out where AI fits in your next twelve months, this is your nudge. And look around while you're at it. Somebody near you is already deciding whether to build this with you or without you.

## 3. We Need to Talk About How Consulting Gets Paid

**Tags:** AI, business, leadership
**Feature/Twitter image:** https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1730789701634-5386e7271462?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wxMjA3fDB8MXxhbGx8fHx8fHx8fHwxNzgwNjcyODE1fA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=2000
**Meta/Twitter description:** Outcome-based pricing is about to replace the hourly model in consulting. Here's why waiting to be asked is the wrong strategy.

The hourly model has been dying for a decade. I think agentic AI just pulled the plug, and most consulting firms, including plenty I respect, are not ready for what comes next.

Bloomberg's estimate stopped me: subscription pricing drops from 60% of software deals to 30% this decade, while outcome-based pricing climbs from 10% to 60%. Read that twice, because it's not a vendor pricing footnote. It's a consulting delivery story hiding inside a software pricing story. If a client's AI agent is resolving support tickets on its own, nobody is paying a human for the hours it took to babysit that queue anymore. They're paying for the outcome. And once a client gets used to paying for outcomes in one part of their business, they start asking why the rest of their vendor relationships don't work the same way. That includes us.

I've sat in enough scoping conversations to know how uncomfortable this shift is going to be. Hourly billing is safe. It's predictable. It's also the exact model a client questions the second they see an agent do in six minutes what used to take a team a week. If your pricing model can't explain that gap, the client will explain it for you, usually in a renewal conversation you didn't see coming.

The firms that figure out outcome-based delivery now, before a client forces the issue, are going to own the next decade of this work. The ones who wait until they're asked will spend that decade playing catch-up on a field their clients designed without them in the room.

So here's the question I keep sitting with, and I'd put it to you too: what would it take to price your next engagement on the outcome instead of the hours? Not a thought experiment for a Friday afternoon. An actual proposal, with numbers on it, that you could put in front of a client next week.

## 4. The $200B Gap Is Yours If You Want It

**Tags:** AI, business
**Feature/Twitter image:** https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1639750066365-48dd5a487a2e?crop=entropy&cs=tinysrgb&fit=max&fm=jpg&ixid=M3wxMjA3fDB8MXxhbGx8fHx8fHx8fHwxNzgzMDM1MzgxfA&ixlib=rb-4.1.0&q=80&w=2000
**Meta/Twitter description:** BCG found $200B in AI services demand the Big 4 are walking away from. Here's why that gap belongs to firms willing to show up.

BCG just put a number on something I've watched happen in real time for the past eighteen months: $200 billion in net new tech services demand from agentic AI, and the Big 4 are walking away from most of it. On purpose.

I don't think it's an accident or an oversight. The large firms are moving upmarket: Fortune 500 accounts, hyperscaler partnerships, platform plays with enough margin to justify their overhead. They're not ignoring the mid-market. They're pricing themselves out of reach by design, because chasing a $200,000 engagement doesn't move the needle for a firm built to close eight figures. That leaves a gap, and gaps like that don't stay empty. Somebody fills them.

That somebody can be firms like ours, and I don't mean that as the consolation prize version of the story. Seventy-five percent of mid-market enterprises say they want a partner to help them build and implement agentic AI, and most of them don't have anyone to call. Not because the work is too small to matter. Because the firms with the loudest brand names decided it wasn't worth their time. At that tier, proximity and trust and how fast you can actually deliver matter more than the name on the slide deck, and that's exactly the ground boutique firms have always tried to compete on.

I keep thinking about how many client relationships I've built specifically because we were responsive when a bigger firm wasn't. That instinct isn't a nice-to-have anymore. It's the entire opportunity BCG just quantified.

So if you're running a firm this size and you're not actively building an agentic AI delivery practice right now, I have to ask what you're waiting for. The window BCG is describing doesn't stay open for firms that show up late to a gap everyone else already noticed.
